Evolution

Nobody designed these.

Beasts are made of cells: eyes that spot food, legs that swim, mouths that eat, brains, and breeders that grow new cells. When two grown beasts meet, their child mixes both bodies, with the odd mutation. Families rise and fall, and the pond fills with shapes nobody planned.

Predators

Hunters in the water.

Tardigrades hunt the beasts. Steer one yourself or let it hunt alone, and add up to eight. Starving hunters curl up into a dormant tun, and then the others eat them. Some beasts swarm: a dense flock confuses the hunters, so loners get eaten first.

Designer

Build a beast, cell by cell.

Design your own creature in the editor and release it into the pond. Keep your favorites: they join every new world. Will your design found a dynasty, or feed the tardigrades?

Stories

Questions

Good to know.

Looking for how to play? See the full help guide.

Is it really free?

Yes. No ads, no purchases, no accounts. Just the pond.

Does it collect any data?

No. The game has no accounts and sends nothing anywhere. The browser version keeps your saves in your own browser. Streaming to YouTube is opt-in and uses your own YouTube account.

Can I play on my phone or iPad?

Yes, in the browser. Hold your phone sideways. On an iPhone, use Share, then Add to Home Screen, and it opens full screen like an app.

Is the evolution real?

Nothing is scripted. Beasts that find food grow and breed. Children mix their parents' cells, with mutations. Predators eat the slow and the alone. What survives is up to the pond, and to you.

Was AI used to make it?

Beasts was built by Andrew R. Harding and was inspired by a conversation with an engineer and mathematician named Simon. Simon wrote an earlier version in Java in the early 2000s. This update was built with some help from a software development agent.

Where does the idea come from?

Andrew was down a rabbit hole researching DIY electronic pets when he found a post from Simon. The two got talking about biosimulation games, and Simon dug out his Java applet from the early 2000s. Andrew rewrote it as an exercise to learn Go. For years since, the beasts have lived in a little pond in Andrew's office, swimming and munching algae, with hardly anyone watching. They're hungry for a little care and feeding.
